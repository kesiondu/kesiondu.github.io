A chibi avatar can look charming at full size and become unreadable beside a chat message. At that scale, a detailed jacket matters less than a clear expression, a distinctive hairstyle, or a strong outline. The design challenge is deciding which details carry the character's identity and which can disappear.

## Pick the destination before you write the prompt

A profile picture has to identify someone in a tight crop. A chat emote has to convey a reaction immediately. A sticker can carry a bigger pose because it usually has more space. Trying to make one image serve all three often leaves you with a head that is too small for chat or limbs that disappear inside a profile crop.

The [Chibi Style photo-to-avatar maker](https://chibistyle.art/) offers a photo or text starting point, use-case options for avatars, Twitch emotes, and Discord stickers, and a four-image preview. That makes it useful for comparing directions within a focused workflow. Its [asset library](https://chibistyle.art/chibi-style-assets) provides examples of how silhouettes and expressions differ between uses. These observations come from the site's public materials; I did not run a generation or test a paid export.

## Preserve three features, not every feature

For a recognizable portrait, start with a sharp, evenly lit reference you have permission to use. Pick two or three traits that survive simplification: a side-swept fringe, round glasses, and a rust-colored scarf, for example. Treat the rest as flexible. A prompt that asks for every seam, pattern, and accessory can bury the features that matter.

Here's an original prompt for a chat emote:

> Create a square chibi emote from the reference portrait. Keep the side-swept fringe, round glasses, and rust scarf. Draw a delighted expression with raised eyebrows and an open smile. Frame the head and shoulders so the face fills most of the canvas. Use broad color shapes, a clear outline, and an empty transparent background. Leave a little space around the head. No words, props, or border.

When comparing variants, shrink all four before choosing. If the smile disappears or the glasses merge into the eyebrows, simplify the face. If the character could be anyone once the image is small, strengthen one identifying feature rather than adding detail everywhere.

## Make the reactions feel like one character

Choose one approved image as a reference for the rest of the set. Keep its palette, face proportions, crop, and outline treatment consistent. Change the expression first. A happy, surprised, and skeptical version should read as three reactions from the same character, not as three different mascots. If a variant drifts, start again from the approved reference rather than using the drifted image as the basis for the next one.

## Check the actual file before uploading

- **Legibility:** Preview the image at the size it will occupy in chat or beside a username. Check a circular crop for profile pictures.
- **Transparency:** Open the PNG over both light and dark backgrounds. Check for white fringes, accidental opaque corners, or a checkerboard baked into the image.
- **Consistency:** Put the reactions side by side. Look for changes in identifying features, relative head size, and framing.
- **Export:** Check the downloaded dimensions and whether your chosen plan includes unmarked files. The site shows a paid mark-removal option, so don't assume the free preview matches the final export.

The best variant is the one you can recognize and interpret at its smallest intended size. Make that decision before spending time polishing the full-size image.
